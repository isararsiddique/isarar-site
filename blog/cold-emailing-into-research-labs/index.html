How I cold-emailed my way into eleven research labs

16 August 2026 · 8 min read

I did my undergraduate degree in biotechnology at a state-affiliated college in Lucknow. It had no research pipeline, no lab I could join, and no faculty connections into the places I wanted to work. Nobody from my course had gone to Harvard or Stanford. There was no path, and pretending otherwise would have wasted a year.

Over the next three years I worked in eleven labs — Harvard, Stanford, NASA GeneLab, the University of Tokyo, ISRO's Physical Research Laboratory, IISc Bangalore, three IITs, and others. I did not go through a single application portal for any of them.

I want to be precise about what happened, because "just cold email people" is useless advice and it isn't what worked.

The first fifty emails failed completely

My early emails looked like everyone's. I introduced myself, listed my CGPA, said I was passionate about the professor's field, attached a CV, and asked whether there were any internship opportunities in their lab.

Reply rate: zero. Not low — zero.

It took me an embarrassingly long time to understand why. That email asks the recipient to do work. It asks them to evaluate me, imagine a project, find funding, and assign supervision — all for a stranger with no track record, from an institution they've never heard of. The rational response is to archive it. I would have archived it.

An ask costs the recipient something. An offer costs them nothing. That distinction is the entire trick.

What I changed

I stopped asking for internships. Instead, for each professor, I did this:

  • Read their three most recent papers properly — not the abstracts, the methods.
  • Find one specific thing: a gap they flagged in their own discussion section, a dataset they'd published but not fully analysed, a method they'd applied to one cohort and not another.
  • Write four or five sentences proposing exactly what I would do about it in the first two weeks. Unpaid. Remote. No supervision required.

That's it. No CV in the first email. No mention of my college. No paragraph about my passion for the field.

Reply rate went to roughly one in six.

Why it works

A specific proposal proves three things at once that a CV cannot. It proves you actually read the work, because you can't fake a genuine gap in someone's methods section. It proves you can scope a piece of work, which is most of what research competence is. And it removes their risk entirely — if I disappeared after a week, they'd lost nothing.

The unpaid part mattered more than I'd like it to. I'm not going to pretend that's a fair system. It meant I could only do this because I had somewhere to live and no dependents. But the mechanism underneath it — make the first move costless for the other person — doesn't require working for free. It requires arriving with something finished.

The part nobody tells you: most of them go nowhere

Eleven labs sounds impressive on a list. In reality several of those were one month long. Some produced nothing publishable. One I discontinued. A few professors stopped replying after the initial project and I never found out why.

The hit rate on meaningful collaborations was much lower than the reply rate. Maybe one in four of the labs I got into turned into work I'd defend today. If you're doing this, expect that, and don't measure yourself on the length of the list.

Using it for something harder

The reason I still think about this isn't academic access. It's that the same move worked on a much harder problem.

When I started Zero Dementia — smartphone-based screening for early cognitive impairment — I needed hospitals. Hospitals are far more conservative than professors. A tertiary institute has no reason to let a 22-year-old near their patients.

So I didn't ask for patients. I built the screening infrastructure first: the recruitment workflow, the consent handling, the data collection pipeline. Then I went to SGPGI Lucknow and two medical colleges with a working system and asked them to tell me what was wrong with it.

That conversation is completely different from asking for access. It's a clinician critiquing a tool, which is something they actively want to do. Those partnerships became the clinical trials that are running now.

If you're going to try this

Three things I'd tell my younger self.

Volume without specificity is spam. Fifty generic emails got me nothing. Ten researched ones got replies. The research is the work; the email is just delivery.

Read the discussion section. That's where researchers write down what they couldn't finish. It is a public list of things someone wants done and doesn't have time for.

Deliver the two weeks you promised, even if they don't reply. Twice I did the analysis anyway and sent it unprompted. Both times that second email is what got the response.

None of this is a substitute for being good at the work. It's a way to get a first look from people who'd otherwise never see you — which, if you're starting from a place with no pipeline, is the only problem that matters at the beginning.
